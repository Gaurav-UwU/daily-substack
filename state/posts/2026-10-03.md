## Why identity matters more than parameters

When GPT‑4 and Claude 2 hit the market, the headline was always the number of parameters. Six months later the same press releases brag about latency improvements or multimodal inputs. The underlying reality is that a production‑grade agent must answer a different question: who is it, and what is it allowed to do?

The moment an agent can be trusted, it becomes a business asset. The moment it cannot, it stays a demo.

## The OpenID specification makes it concrete

Last week OpenID published the first formal "Identity Management for Agentic AI" specification. The document turns a series of blog posts into a standards track that defines how an autonomous system presents a verifiable identifier, negotiates scopes, and revokes credentials. It is the first industry‑wide agreement that identity, not model size, will be the gate‑keeper for AI services.

The specification introduces three primitives that any agent platform must support. First, an OpenID‑based identifier that can be resolved to a decentralized DID. Second, verifiable credentials that bind the identifier to capabilities such as "access to user calendar" or "ability to execute financial trades". Third, a fine‑grained delegation model that lets a user grant a temporary token to an agent and revoke it on demand. All three are defined in a way that works across clouds, on‑prem, and edge devices.

## Turning identity into a product moat

Most roadmaps still treat authentication as a checkbox. Embedding the OpenID flow turns that checkbox into a revenue‑generating feature. A SaaS platform can charge per credential issuance, per delegation event, or per revocation audit.

Because the credential is cryptographically bound to the agent, a competitor cannot simply copy the model and sell it under a different name without rebuilding the entire identity stack.

## Economic and regulatory benefits

Compute costs fall as hardware improves and as model distillation matures. Identity infrastructure, on the other hand, scales linearly with the number of active agents and the frequency of credential checks. That makes it a predictable cost center and a lever for unit‑economics optimization. Pricing per credential or per delegation creates a clear path to profitability that does not depend on the volatile GPU market.

Financial regulators are already demanding proof of intent for automated trading bots. Healthcare compliance frameworks require auditable consent for any data‑driven decision. By issuing verifiable credentials that encode consent scopes, an agent can demonstrate compliance in real time. The spec’s revocation mechanism also satisfies the "right to be forgotten" requirement without tearing down the underlying model.

## Action steps and competitive outlook

**What a product manager should do on Monday**

1. Audit every external API call your agents make and map it to a required capability.
2. Draft a minimal credential schema that covers those capabilities.
3. Prototype the OpenID flow using the reference implementation linked in the specification.
4. Set up a revocation endpoint and log the audit trail.

Early adopters like Anthropic and Microsoft have filed patents that reference verifiable credentials for their agents. Start‑ups that ignore the OpenID spec will find themselves blocked by enterprise procurement teams that demand auditable identity. The moat will not be the size of the model but the breadth of the credential ecosystem you have built.

**Takeaway**

The next wave of autonomous AI will be judged on who can prove its authority, not on how many parameters it can squeeze into a GPU. Embedding OpenID‑based identity, verifiable credentials, and delegation today creates a lock‑in that protects against commoditization, reduces regulatory exposure, and opens a new revenue stream. If you wait for the market to force you, you will be building on sand.
