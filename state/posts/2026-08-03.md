## The Regret That Started a Revolution

When I joined Whatnot, the first thing I said to the team was that we regret product management exists. It sounds like a punchline, but the point is simple: most PM structures exist to fill seats, not to ship impact. Whatnot grew to over $8 billion in GMV on a live-shopping model, yet the engine behind that growth was a handful of senior product leaders who still write specs and ship features themselves.

The mantra forced us to ask: are we hiring PMs to manage other PMs, or to solve real user problems? The answer reshaped every hiring rubric and forced us to discard the classic “one PM per two engineers” rule.

## Senior ICs Over Ratio-Driven Teams

At Twitch and Twitter I watched org charts balloon while velocity stalled. The obvious fix was more managers, more layers, more process. Whatnot tried the opposite: we kept the headcount low and let senior PMs stay in the trenches. The result was a team that could iterate on a new auction flow in weeks instead of months.

The mental model I call “play the accordion” captures this. You stretch the team only when the market forces demand a new feature set, then you compress it back to the core senior talent. This elasticity is impossible when you have a rigid hierarchy of junior PMs waiting for direction.

## AI as the Force Multiplier

AI didn’t just change how we collect data; it changed who can interpret it. At Whatnot we built a data-science pipeline that surfaces buyer intent in real time, and senior PMs use those signals directly to prioritize experiments. The same AI tools that once required a dedicated analyst now sit on the PM’s dashboard.

Because senior PMs own the end-to-end loop, they can ask the right questions of the model and act on the answer without translation. The result is a feedback cycle that is both faster and more accurate than any layered org could achieve.

## Hiring for Depth, Not Numbers

When I interview, I look for two things: a track record of shipping high-impact features and the willingness to stay hands-on. A senior PM at Whatnot still writes user stories, runs A/B tests, and dives into SQL. If they can’t do the work themselves, they’re not a fit.

The old “hire great people and get out of their way” approach fails because it assumes seniority equals delegation. In practice, senior PMs who stay on the front line keep the product vision grounded and prevent the drift that comes from abstract management.

## The Future of Product Work

Start the week by pulling the latest AI-driven insights from the data dashboard. Identify the top three hypotheses that could move GMV, and schedule a rapid experiment for the most promising one. Then, spend the afternoon coding a prototype or writing the exact acceptance criteria for the engineering squad.

If you find yourself drafting a status report instead of building, you’re in the wrong role. The future of product work is not about coordinating meetings; it’s about delivering measurable outcomes with a small, expert team.

## The Moat That Emerges

Depth creates a moat that scale cannot replicate. When senior PMs own the full product loop, knowledge stays inside the team and is not diluted across layers. Competitors can copy a feature list, but they cannot copy the tacit expertise that lets a five-person team out-move a fifty-person org.

In the AI era, that expertise is amplified. The same model that predicts a user’s next bid can be tuned by a senior PM in minutes, whereas a larger org would need weeks of coordination.

Within the next two years, the average product org at high-growth consumer startups will shrink its PM headcount by at least 30 percent, replacing the missing capacity with AI-augmented senior ICs. The metric that will matter most will be impact per PM, not PMs per engineer.

If you’re still building a pyramid of junior managers, you’re building for a world that no longer exists.
