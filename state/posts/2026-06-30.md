There is a story going around about Meta, and it is almost too perfect to be true. The company, in a push to get everyone using AI, tied performance evaluations to token consumption. The result was predictable and, depending on your sense of humor, hilarious. Engineers had two agents talking to each other all day, burning through tokens like a whale on a gacha game, just to hit their numbers.

Most people looked at this and saw a face-palm. Executives, in their infinite wisdom, accidentally lit a pile of money on fire. But I think that take is wrong, or at least incomplete. It wasn't an accident. It was a blunt-force instrument. A few months ago, a lot of senior, respected people inside organizations were deeply resistant to using AI tools at all. They would use them in ways that guaranteed weird or bad outcomes, then point to the failure as proof. The top-down tokenmaxxing policy was a way to break through that wall. Yes, it was wasteful. But sometimes you need a sledgehammer to crack a nut when the nut is organizational inertia.

That was then. The wall has been breached. Everyone is using AI to code now, at least a little. Cursor lives in the sidebar. The holdouts have folded. Token spend, naturally, has gone way up. And right on cue, the economics are shifting underneath us. Both OpenAI and Anthropic are eyeing public markets. The era of heavily subsidized tokens is ending. Subscription limits are tightening, API prices are climbing, and teams are starting to roll back their unlimited-spend policies. The party, it seems, is over. Tokenmaxxing is dead.

Except that it is not. It is just shape-shifting.

## The End of the Firehose

The promise of AI agents is that they can run without human supervision to accomplish hard, tedious tasks. The big code migration. The daily competitive research sweep. Triaging the firehose of inbound and outbound. These are things people hate doing and desperately want AI to do. Up until recently, you couldn't reliably have an agent run for long periods. The context window would overflow, the model would lose the plot, and you would come back to a hallucinated mess. The solution, in the tokenmaxxing era, was brute force. Just give the model a bigger window. Cram everything in. Burn tokens like they were free, because for a brief moment, they practically were.

That brute force approach is a dead end walking. Not because the technical ceiling on context windows has been reached, but because the economic floor has fallen out. You can't just throw a million tokens at a problem and hope for the best when every token is being priced for profit. The cost structure is forcing a shift from a quantity mindset to a quality one. The question is no longer "How big is your context window?" It is "What is your context strategy?"

## The New Moat: Context Middleware

This is where the real product work begins. The winners in the next phase will not be the companies with the biggest models. They will be the ones that build the most intelligent layer between the task and the model. Call it context middleware. Its job is to structure, compress, and retrieve information so that an agent can operate reliably within a fixed token budget.

Think of it like packing for a month-long trip. The old way was to rent a shipping container and throw in your entire house. The new way is to become a master of the carry-on. You need to know exactly what is essential, how to fold it so it takes up minimal space, and how to access it quickly without unpacking everything. For an AI agent, this means systems that can summarize long conversation histories without losing critical state. It means retrieval mechanisms that pull in only the most relevant documents, not the entire corpus. It means compressing instructions and memories into dense, high-signal formats that leave the model's attention uncluttered for the actual reasoning work.

This is not a marginal optimization. It is the difference between an agent that works and one that is a parlor trick. A coding agent that can hold an entire codebase in its effective memory, not by brute-force dumping it into the prompt, but by intelligently indexing and retrieving the right files at the right time, is a different species of tool. A customer support agent that can recall the full nuance of a six-month enterprise negotiation without re-reading every transcript is not a cost center; it is a competitive advantage.

## What a PM Should Do on Monday

The commoditization of the large language model is happening in real time. The model itself is becoming the predictable input cost, like cloud compute. The unpredictable, and therefore defensible, value is in the systems built around it. If you are a product manager or a founder, stop obsessing over which model has the highest benchmark score. Start obsessing over your information architecture.

Ask your team: What is our context strategy? How do we decide what the agent remembers and what it forgets? How do we ensure that the most valuable piece of information is always within the model's attention budget when it matters most? The companies that answer these questions with elegant, systematic solutions will build products that feel like magic. The ones that don't will just be burning tokens in a more sophisticated way, wondering why their margins are evaporating. The sledgehammer had its moment. Now it is time for a scalpel.
