Most AI products are built on a lie. We act as if LLMs think like humans, reasoning, remembering, intending. We call them agents. We expect them to hold context. We blame them when they forget. But here’s the truth: LLMs have no inner life. Words are not the output of thought. For them, words are the only thing that exists. Devarsh Ranpara puts it plainly: for humans, words are a byproduct of consciousness. First comes the idea, the feeling, the image. Then, we hunt for the right words to express it. For an LLM, it’s the reverse. There is no idea. No memory. No self. Only a statistical prediction: given these words, what comes next? This isn’t a flaw. It’s the architecture. Every call to an LLM begins from a blank slate. The model sees only what you put in the prompt, nothing more, nothing less. When ChatGPT seems to remember your last message, it’s not recalling. It’s being reminded. The platform injected your prior exchange into the context window. That’s not memory. That’s scaffolding. And yet, we build products as if the model has continuity. We design AI agents that are supposed to ‘keep track’ of user goals. We expect them to learn our preferences over time. We treat them like assistants, not tools. The result? Product fatigue. Users get frustrated not because the model hallucinates facts, they get frustrated because the system keeps pretending it understands them. The real danger isn’t factual errors. It’s projection. Humans are wired to see agency in patterns. We anthropomorphize thermostats. We scold GPS devices. Now we’re doing it to language models that have zero internal state. We assign them intentions they don’t have. We trust them with workflows they can’t sustain. And when they fail, because they were never designed to hold a thread, we call it a bug. It’s not. It’s a design error. Look at AI agents in the wild. They’re built on the assumption that context can be preserved, goals can be tracked, and memory can be inferred. But every time you send a new request, the model resets. The system must artificially reconstruct the past by pasting it into the prompt. That’s expensive. That’s fragile. That’s why agents forget mid-conversation. Not because they’re dumb. Because they’re stateless by design. The most AI-native PMs aren’t trying to make LLMs think. They’re building interfaces that compensate for the absence of thought. They’re not asking the model to remember. They’re asking the product to remember for it. Take Greptile. It doesn’t rely on Claude to remember code conventions. It runs the code. It executes changes in a sandbox. It returns logs, screenshots, traces. The model doesn’t need to recall your team’s style, it just sees the proof of what broke. The memory lives in the system, not the model. Or consider Lenny’s framework: the highest rung of AI leverage isn’t delegating tasks to LLMs. It’s building systems where AI completes multi-step tasks and checks its own results. But notice what’s missing from that stack? The LLM isn’t the keeper of state. The product is. The evals. The sandbox. The versioned context. The test suite. That’s where the intelligence lives now. The future of AI product design isn’t about making models smarter. It’s about making the layers around them smarter. Your job as a PM isn’t to train the model to remember. It’s to build the scaffolding that lets it appear to. This changes everything. Stop designing for consciousness. Start designing for context injection. Stop trusting the model to hold the thread. Start treating conversation history like a database query. Stop blaming the AI for forgetting. Start building systems that never let it forget in the first place. The next great AI product won’t be the one with the most advanced model. It’ll be the one that hides the statelessness the best. The real question isn’t whether your AI agent can remember. It’s whether your product can remember for it.
