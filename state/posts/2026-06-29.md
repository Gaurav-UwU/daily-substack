We have been optimizing the wrong thing.

The conversation around AI in product development is stuck on code generation. Can it write a function faster? Can it scaffold a component? This misses the point entirely. The real bottleneck in software has never been typing speed. It has been the latency of human-to-human context transfer. The meetings. The tickets. The specs that are out of date the moment they are written. The coordination tax.

Eddie Kim, the CTO of Gusto, just provided the most concrete evidence yet that this tax is now optional. He didn't just use AI to write code. He used it to fundamentally change the operating system of a product team. The result was a net-new product line, Gusto Cofounder, built from zero to a tier-one launch in 10 weeks by a team of five people: three engineers, one designer, and Eddie himself, who went back to writing code.

The process they used is a radical departure from the religion of modern product development. No Figma. No Jira. No PRDs. No standups. No retros. No Slack threads. They replaced all of it with two things: Claude Code and a permanent Zoom call.

## The Death of the Artifact

In a traditional team, artifacts are the primary mechanism for transferring context. A PM writes a PRD to transfer context to a designer. A designer creates Figma mocks to transfer context to an engineer. An engineer writes a technical spec to transfer context to another engineer. Each handoff is a game of telephone with a latency measured in days.

Gusto's team collapsed this chain by making the conversation the artifact. The "perma-Zoom" was an always-on video call that served as the team's persistent workspace. If you had a question, you just asked. If a decision was made, everyone heard it in real time. There was no need to document the decision because the decision itself was the coordination event. The latency of context transfer dropped from hours or days to seconds.

This only works if the cost of acting on that context is also near zero. That's where Claude Code comes in. In a world where a designer with no engineering background can hit the 94th percentile for shipping code, the person who holds the context can also execute on it. The loop is closed. The handoff disappears.

## The Trash-Can Method

Eddie described a specific tactic they called the "trash-can method" for product decisions. Instead of writing a planning document that attempts to predict the future, they would write a full PR as if the feature already existed. Then they would review it. Then they would delete it.

The PR served as the product decision. It forced the team to think through the complete shape of the solution, from the user-facing narrative down to the edge cases, without the overhead of maintaining a living document. The act of writing and discarding was the thinking process. The real artifact was the shared understanding created during the exercise, not the document itself.

This is a first-principles attack on documentation. We write docs to align people. If the team is already aligned through continuous, high-bandwidth communication, the doc is waste. It's a hedge against forgetting. But if the team is small, co-located on a permanent call, and shipping multiple times a day, the half-life of a document is measured in hours. The cost of maintaining it exceeds the value of having it.

## What Gets Commoditized

If this model spreads, and I believe it will, the implications for product roles are significant. The coordination work that has defined the PM role for two decades, writing tickets, managing backlogs, facilitating handoffs, chasing status updates, gets commoditized by this pattern. When a team is in continuous sync and AI can translate intent directly into working software, the project manager function is absorbed into the team's ambient communication.

What becomes scarce is taste and judgment. The designer on Gusto's team didn't become valuable because they learned to prompt an AI. They became valuable because they had a clear vision for what the product should feel like and could now execute on that vision directly. The leverage moved from coordination to creation.

For engineers, the leverage moves from implementation to evaluation. Eddie described an "eval-first workflow" for fixing real customer bugs with Claude Code. The skill isn't writing the fix. The skill is defining the right test, the right eval, that proves the fix works. The engineer becomes a judge of quality, not a producer of syntax.

## The Replicable Blueprint

This isn't a one-off experiment by a startup with nothing to lose. Gusto just crossed $1 billion in revenue and serves more than 500,000 small businesses. This is a public company's CTO giving a detailed, replicable blueprint for a new way to build.

The model is deceptively simple: a small team, a permanent communication channel, and an AI-native toolchain that lets anyone on the team turn intent into code. The stack they used is not exotic: Claude Code, Cloudflare Workers, the Vercel AI SDK. The innovation is not the technology. It's the willingness to throw out the process religion and ask what is actually necessary for a group of smart people to ship valuable software together.

The answer, it turns out, is very little. And that should terrify anyone whose job description is built on managing the coordination tax.
