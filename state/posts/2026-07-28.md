## The pipeline in plain sight

A tweet from HedgieMarkets exposed a supply chain that reads like a sci-fi heist. AI firms are bulk-buying pre-2022 books, feeding them to high-speed scanners that slice the spines off, then shredding the originals. The service, ISBNdb, can order up to a million titles and hides buyers behind NDAs. A federal judge has ruled the practice fair use because the physical copy disappears, leaving only a digital version.

The same thread notes that Anthropic hired the former head of Google Books partnerships with a single mandate: "all the books in the world." The goal is not preservation but a data moat that fuels better marketing emails, chat replies, and code suggestions. The irony is that the only thing being preserved is a model that can now regurgitate the text.

## Fair use, anonymity, and the new supply chain

Legal scholars have long debated the line between digitization and theft. The judge’s decision sidesteps the moral question by focusing on the fact that only one copy exists at a time. ISBNdb’s own website admits that "AI company destroys two million books" does not generate sympathy, yet it has built a business around the quiet destruction of cultural artifacts.

Anonymity is baked into the model. Buyers sign NDAs, and the service markets the act as "digital preservation." The language masks the irreversible loss of unique items, think of an 18th-century botanical text with three surviving copies. Once those are shredded, no amount of re-printing can restore the original paper, marginalia, and binding.

## The strategic trade-off behind the shredders

From a product perspective, the move is a classic cost-benefit calculation. Physical books are a scarce resource, but they are also a high-margin input for training data. By converting them to pixels, firms turn a rare asset into a cheap, replicable one. The price of the scanner and the legal shield are negligible compared to the competitive advantage of a model that can cite obscure sources.

The trade-off is not just financial. Companies are choosing to erase the provenance of knowledge, betting that the model’s output will be sufficient for users. That bet shifts the moat from data ownership to model sophistication. As more firms adopt the same pipeline, the marginal value of each new scanned volume drops, turning the practice into a race to consume the remaining rarest titles before they disappear.

## What PMs should do on Monday

If you are building a product that relies on AI-generated content, ask yourself three questions: (1) does your value proposition depend on the uniqueness of the source material? (2) can you afford the reputational risk of being linked to irreversible cultural loss? (3) is there a cheaper, less destructive way to acquire the same signal, such as licensing public-domain scans or partnering with libraries that already digitize their collections?

A practical framework is to map each data source on a two-axis grid: scarcity versus replaceability. Rare, non-replaceable books land in the high-risk quadrant and should be avoided unless the product truly needs that niche knowledge. Replaceable texts, modern textbooks, bestsellers, can be sourced through conventional channels without the ethical baggage.

## A glimpse of the next decade

The shredding trend will accelerate until the last irreplaceable volumes are gone. At that point, the competitive edge will shift from raw data volume to how cleverly a model can synthesize existing digital corpora. Companies that double down on destructive acquisition may find themselves with a hollow moat, one that can be eroded by any firm that respects preservation and still builds strong embeddings.

The deeper lesson is that AI progress is no longer just a race for compute; it is a race for the right kind of knowledge. The strategic shift we are witnessing forces product leaders to treat physical artifacts as a finite resource, not an endless well. The question for every PM now is whether you will build on a foundation of burnt books or on a sustainable ecosystem of openly shared knowledge.
