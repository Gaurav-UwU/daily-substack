## The Shift from On‑Demand to Persistent

When OpenAI released Dots on September 29, 2026 the headline was simple: an always‑on AI that lives in the background of a user’s workflow. The novelty is not the model itself, but the fact that the assistant no longer waits for a prompt. It watches calendars, drafts replies, and nudges users before a meeting even starts. That change flips the mental model from “call a function when you need it” to “run a service that constantly consumes compute”.

Product teams that built ChatGPT plugins or one‑off API calls now have to think in terms of a living process. The engineering effort moves from scaling request spikes to guaranteeing steady‑state performance. In practice that means re‑architecting back‑ends to handle long‑lived sockets, state snapshots, and graceful restarts. The shift is subtle but profound: the product becomes a platform that must stay alive, not a tool that is invoked.

## Pricing the Clock

Historically we priced AI on a per‑token or per‑call basis because the cost driver was inference time. Dots forces a new unit of account: compute‑seconds. A user who runs an assistant for eight hours a day will pay for the same amount of GPU cycles whether they ask one question or a hundred. That creates a direct incentive to make the assistant efficient in its idle state.

For PMs the immediate question is how to surface that cost to customers without scaring them off. One approach is a tiered model where a base quota of background minutes is free, and excess usage is billed at a lower rate than on‑demand calls. Another is to bundle Dots into a subscription that guarantees a certain uptime SLA. The key insight is that the pricing lever is now a trade‑off between latency, persistence, and safety, not just model size.

## Control and Trust

A background agent that can read emails, calendar events, and drafts raises an immediate privacy red flag. The Hacker News thread titled “What will openai get in return, what about user privacy?” (December 18, 2024) shows that the community is already skeptical about data collection. With Dots the surface area for leakage expands dramatically because the assistant is always listening.

Product managers must therefore bake granular consent flows into the UI. Users should be able to toggle which data streams are exposed, set time windows for active monitoring, and revoke access instantly. From an engineering perspective that means building audit logs that can be exported on demand, and encryption that protects state both at rest and in motion. The trust equation becomes a core feature, not an afterthought.

## The New Moat: Uptime, State, and Safety

In the early days of LLMs the competitive edge was model scale and prompt engineering. With Dots the battlefield moves to operational excellence. An assistant that crashes at 2 am or loses context after a reboot is useless, regardless of how clever its responses are. Uptime therefore becomes a moat that is hard to copy.

State‑management is the second pillar. Dots must retain conversation history, task progress, and user preferences across sessions. Companies that invest in a durable state store, versioned schemas, and conflict‑resolution logic will deliver a smoother experience.

Safety mechanisms that prevent the agent from taking unintended actions become a differentiator. Real‑time guardrails, policy‑driven action limits, and human‑in‑the‑loop overrides will separate the trustworthy from the risky.

## What PMs Do Tomorrow

On Monday a product manager should start by mapping every user interaction that Dots could automate and estimating the compute‑second cost of each. Next, draft a consent matrix that lists data sources, activation windows, and revocation paths. Then, prototype a dashboard that shows live usage and lets users set hard limits on background minutes.

Finally, run a small‑scale pilot with a handful of power users. Measure crash rates, state drift, and privacy complaints. The data will tell you whether the new moat of uptime and safety is delivering enough value to justify the engineering investment.

If the pilot shows that users are willing to pay for a reliable, privacy‑first background assistant, the business case for Dots becomes clear. A successful pilot can also provide concrete metrics for pricing and marketing strategies.

**Takeaway:** Persistent AI agents turn the product from a query engine into a continuously running service. The real competitive advantage will be the ability to keep that service alive, safe, and respectful of user data. PMs who lock down uptime, state, and privacy today will own the next generation of AI‑powered products.
