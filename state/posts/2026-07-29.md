## The intent gap in food search

Type “something healthy for a rainy evening” into a delivery app and the result is usually a mix of pizza, soup, and a salad you never heard of. Five years ago the same query would have returned a random jumble of items because keyword search treats the words as a bag of tokens. The gap isn’t just about synonyms or typos; it’s about translating a vague human intent into a concrete set of SKUs that satisfy dietary constraints, location, and price.

The failure modes are well-known: “soda” vs “soft drink”, misspelling “mozzarela”, shorthand like “gf pizza”, bilingual words such as Spanish “pan”, and word-sense clashes like Apple the fruit versus Apple the company. Beneath those lies the long-tail of unique queries and hard constraints (vegan, gluten-free, allergen-free) that a pure similarity engine can’t honor. Food delivery platforms are a perfect proving ground because every one of those edge cases shows up in the same search bar.

## DoorDash: LLM as a semantic front-end to a knowledge graph

DoorDash entered the LLM era with an existing knowledge graph that already linked items to restaurants, categories, and attributes. The team chose to let the model sit in front of that graph, using it to rewrite the user’s natural language into a structured query that the graph could consume. In practice, the LLM parses “something healthy for a rainy evening” into constraints, low-calorie, comfort-type, indoor-delivery, and hands those to the graph which then returns a deterministic set of matches.

Because the graph already encoded inventory, pricing, and dietary tags, the LLM never had to reason about inventory scarcity or price fluctuations. The heavy lifting stayed in the graph’s fast lookup engine, while the model handled the ambiguous language. For a product manager, the Monday-to-Friday workflow is clear: maintain the graph’s fidelity, monitor LLM prompt quality, and you get a system that scales with catalog growth without re-training the model for every new SKU.

## Instacart: LLM-generated candidates filtered by constraint logic

Instacart’s catalog is dominated by grocery items, many of which lack the clean hierarchical tags that a knowledge graph provides. The company therefore let the LLM generate a shortlist of candidates directly from the query, then applied a separate constraint engine to enforce dietary filters, stock levels, and price caps. In other words, the model acts as a creative searcher, surfacing items that a keyword matcher would miss, while a downstream rule-based filter guarantees compliance.

This architecture pushes the LLM deeper into the runtime, it participates in candidate generation, but still isolates the hard business rules in a deterministic layer. The product implication is that you can iterate on the prompt or model size without fearing accidental violations of allergy policies; the constraint engine is the safety net. Instacart’s engineers can focus on improving the LLM’s recall for the long tail while the rule engine handles the “must-have” checks.

## Uber Eats: LLM as a reranker on top of traditional retrieval

Uber Eats kept its legacy keyword engine because it already delivered low-latency results for the bulk of queries. The LLM was introduced as a reranker: the classic engine returns a candidate pool, the model scores each candidate against the original natural language intent, and the top-scoring items surface to the user. This shallow integration lets Uber preserve its existing infrastructure while gaining a semantic boost for ambiguous queries.

The reranker approach is the least invasive. It requires only an API call to the model after the initial retrieval, meaning the engineering effort is bounded and the latency impact is predictable. For a PM, the focus shifts to monitoring the reranker's lift on conversion metrics and tuning the cutoff size of the candidate pool. The core search stack remains untouched, which is attractive when the product roadmap is already packed with other features.

## What the three choices reveal about AI strategy

The three architectures map cleanly onto a simple decision framework: How deep should the LLM reach into the runtime?

- Deep front-end (DoorDash) works when you have a rich, queryable knowledge base and need to translate fuzzy intent into precise filters.

- Mid-layer generation + rule filter (Instacart) suits catalogs where hierarchical metadata is sparse but business constraints are non-negotiable.

- Shallow rerank (Uber Eats) fits organizations that want immediate lift without re-architecting their core search engine.

The choice is less about the model itself and more about the surrounding infrastructure. DoorDash could lean on its graph, Instacart on its constraint engine, Uber on its fast keyword index. That means the real moat is the data plumbing you already own, not the LLM you plug in.

For product leaders, the takeaway is clear: audit your existing search stack, identify where the intent-to-catalog translation breaks, and then decide how far the LLM needs to go to fix that break. A half-baked integration that only rewrites queries without addressing constraints will produce flashy demos but brittle production.

## A prediction for the next wave

As more verticals adopt LLM-enhanced search, I expect a convergence toward hybrid pipelines: a fast lexical retriever, a generative candidate layer, and a deterministic constraint validator. The companies that already own two of those pieces will simply insert the missing link. The real competitive advantage will be the ability to swap models in and out while keeping the surrounding pipeline stable, a capability that today’s monolithic LLM deployments lack.
